The five marked letters

Five letters, four sounds, one sitting. After this you can sound out the first line of the Lord's Prayer.

25 minNo audio neededDrill inside

Romanian is kinder to readers than English ever was. Its spelling is highly phonemic: a letter makes its sound and keeps making it. English gave us though, through and tough. Romanian would never.

The alphabet has 31 letters: the 26 you know, plus five with marks. Those five are the whole problem, and the whole lesson.

Why the marks felt slippery. Small type and thin fonts make a comma under an s nearly invisible. Everything Romanian in this course is set large, in a typeface built for telling letters apart, with the five marked letters in red. The switch for the red is in the top bar.

The one with no English match

The sound of â and î is the only stranger here. In my experience the quickest route is this: say “ee”, keep your lips exactly where they are, and pull your tongue straight back. The dull vowel that comes out is close.

You don't need to produce it well yet. You need to recognise it on the page.

Why two letters for one sound?

Position. Î goes at the start and end of a word; â takes the middle. So în (in) opens with î, and pâine (bread) carries â inside. The rule has a few wrinkles, and none of them change how you read.

Older books break this rule. Earlier printings write that sound as î everywhere. An older edition of Matthew 6 prints pămînt and pînea where a current one prints pământ and pâinea. Same words, same sounds. If a pew Bible looks odd, this is usually why.

The marks change the word

These are letters in their own right. Drop one and you may have written a different word. Guess each pair, then tap to check.

Tap a word to see its meaning

Look again at casa and casă. That one small mark is the difference between the house and a house. Romanian hangs “the” on the end of the noun, and week two is about exactly that.

A fisherman who writes peste on his shop sign is selling “over”. Business is slow.

A wrinkle you will meet online

You will sometimes see ş and ţ with a small hook where the comma should be. Early computer standards gave Romanian the wrong shape, and the comma forms only reached Unicode in 1999, so a great deal of older text still carries the hook.

Read both as the same letter. And when a text has no marks at all, which happens in chat messages, the words are still there; you just lean on context.

Now read something real

Check yourself

The six: ă in Tatăl, ș in ești, î in în, ț and ă in Sfințească, ă in Tău.

Word for word: The-Father our who are in heavens! Be-made-holy the-Name Your.

A rough sounding-out, good enough for today: TAH-tuhl NOS-troo KAH-reh yesht uhn CHEH-roor. The “ch” in ceruri and the clipped ending of ești are the next two lessons.

The whole prayer is waiting in the reader, with every word tappable.
