C and G, the letters that change their minds

Two letters have two sounds each. One table sorts them out for good.

25 minNo audio neededDrill inside

Yesterday's promise was that a Romanian letter makes one sound and keeps making it. Two letters break that promise, and they are the only two whose sound depends on what follows: c and g.

The good news is that they break it by rule. Learn one small table and every c and g in the language is settled.

The rule

Before e or i, both letters go soft. Everywhere else they stay hard.

You seeYou sayExamples
c + a, o, u, or a consonantkcasă house · cu with · cred I believe
c + e, ich, as in churchcer heaven · cine who
ch + e, ikchem I call · ochi eye
g + a, o, u, or a consonantg, as in gogură mouth · glas voice
g + e, ij, as in gemger frost · înger angel
gh + e, ig, as in goghid guide · Gheorghe George

The trap for English eyes. Romanian ch is a hard k, never the sound in “church”. The h makes no sound of its own. Its whole job is to stand between the c and the vowel and keep the c hard.

Think of the h as a bodyguard. Nobody hears him. Nobody softens the c while he's there.

The vowel that only softens

Sometimes an e or i sits after c or g purely to make it soft, and then steps aside for the next vowel. The same machinery runs in Italian, where ciao is “chow”.

So cea in the Lord's Prayer is close to “cha”, and rugăciune, the word for prayer, is roo-guh-CHOO-neh. Four syllables, with no separate “ee”.

The rest of the alphabet in ninety seconds

Everything else behaves. A handful of letters differ from English habit:

  • j is the “zh” in azure: ajutor, help, is ah-zhoo-TOR.
  • r is a tap or a trill. The quick tap in American butter is close enough.
  • h is always sounded when it stands alone, as in behind: har, grace.
  • The vowels stay pure: e as in let, i as in machine, a as in father, u as “oo”.
